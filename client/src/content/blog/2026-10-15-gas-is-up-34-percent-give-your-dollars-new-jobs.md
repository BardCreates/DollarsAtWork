Prices rose 3.7% over the past year, according to the inflation report released this week. That sounds manageable. Then you fill your tank. Gasoline costs 34% more than it did a year ago, and when one line of your budget jumps like that, the money has to come from somewhere. The only question is whether you pick where, or your credit card does.

### What the September numbers say

The Bureau of Labor Statistics released its September Consumer Price Index on October 14. Prices rose 3.7% over 12 months, up from 3.4% in August. In September alone they rose 0.6%.

Energy did most of the lifting. Energy prices are up 20.4% over the year. Gasoline rose 9.1% in a single month and 34.2% from a year earlier. CBS News reports that gas made up more than half of September's increase, and ties the spike to the wars involving Iran and Ukraine. One market analyst summed it up for CBS: "The CPI was clearly hot, and there aren't many ways to sugarcoat it."

Not everything is soaring, though. Groceries are up 1.8% over the year. Restaurant meals are up 3.5% and housing costs 3.2%. Core inflation, which leaves out food and energy, sits at 2.6%.

### Why your budget feels more than 3.7%

The 3.7% is an average across everything Americans buy. Your budget is not average. If you drive 40 miles a day to work, gas is a bigger slice of your spending than it is of the national basket, so the 34% hits you harder. If you work from home, you may barely notice.

Here is what that looks like in dollars. A household that spent $200 a month on gas last fall would spend about $268 today at 34% higher prices. That is $68 more a month, or about $816 a year. (Example numbers. Yours will differ.)

In most budgets, that $68 does not come from a decision. It comes from whatever is left over at the end of the month. For a lot of people paying down debt, the leftover is the extra payment.

### The quiet cost: debt is getting pricier too

Inflation is not the only thing moving. The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate on September 16 to a range of 3.75% to 4%, its first increase since 2023. After this week's report, CBS notes that markets see about even odds of another hike at the Fed's October 27–28 meeting.

Most credit cards carry variable rates that follow the Fed's moves. Forbes Advisor's weekly survey put the average card APR at 24.95% as of October 12.

Now put the two together. Say you owe $8,000 on a card at 24.95% and you have been paying $400 a month. Gas quietly takes $68 of that, and your payment drops to $332.

| Monthly payment | Months to pay off | Total interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $400 | 27 | about $2,450 |
| $332 (gas took $68) | 34 | about $3,220 |

That $68 turns into 7 more months of debt and about $770 more in interest. (Example. Assumes no new charges and a steady rate.) That is the real price of letting inflation make your budget decisions for you.

### A 20-minute re-plan for higher prices

Zero-based budgeting means every dollar of income gets a job before the month starts. When prices move, you do not throw out the plan. You reassign jobs on purpose.

1. **Update the categories that moved.** Compare last month's gas, utilities and travel against what you planned. Use the numbers from your statements, not the numbers you hoped for.
2. **Find the gap.** Add up how much more those categories need. That one number is what you have to cover.
3. **Choose where it comes from.** Look for categories with some give this season: dining out, subscriptions, a gift fund that is ahead of schedule. Move the dollars yourself instead of letting the month decide.
4. **Protect the debt payment if you can.** A snowball only rolls if the extra payment shows up every month. If it has to shrink, shrink it by a set amount and write down the month it goes back up.
5. **Shrink the gas line itself.** Batch errands into one trip, keep tires properly inflated, compare station prices, and share a ride a day or two a week. Every dollar you save goes straight back to another category.
6. **Set a review date.** Energy prices can fall as fast as they rose. Put next month's check-in on the calendar and move dollars back the moment they free up.

### If gas comes down, give the money a job right away

The flip side matters just as much. If prices ease this winter, the extra room will not go anywhere useful on its own. It will drift into everyday spending. The day you notice it, send it back to the debt payment or into your emergency fund.
