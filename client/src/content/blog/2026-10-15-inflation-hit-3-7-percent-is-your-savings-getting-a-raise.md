Every dollar in your savings account has a job: sit tight and be there when you need it. But this week's inflation report raises a fair question. While those dollars wait, are they keeping up with prices? If your money sits at a typical bank rate, the answer right now is no. Each month it can buy a little less than the month before.

The good news: this is one of the easier money problems to fix, and it might take you an afternoon.

### What changed this week

On October 14, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that consumer prices rose 3.7% over the 12 months ending in September, up from 3.4% in August. Energy drove most of the jump, with gasoline up 34% from a year earlier.

Interest rates are moving too. On September 16, the Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate to a range of 3.75% to 4%, its first increase since 2023. CBS News reports that markets now see about even odds of another hike at the Fed's October 27–28 meeting.

That matters for savers because bank savings rates tend to follow the Fed. Online banks usually move first. Fortune's daily rate roundup on October 15 found top high-yield savings accounts paying up to 4.50% APY, with many near or above 4%.

Meanwhile, the FDIC's national average savings rate is 0.37%. That is the average across all banks, including big branch banks where many people keep their savings out of habit.

### The math: what 3.7% does to $10,000

Inflation is the quiet cost of holding cash. Your balance doesn't shrink, but what it can buy does. To see whether your savings are actually growing, compare the rate you earn with the inflation rate.

Here is an example emergency fund of $10,000, held for one year, if prices keep rising 3.7%:

| Where it sits | Interest earned in a year | Buying power after inflation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Account at 0.37% (national average) | about $37 | about $9,680, a loss of $320 |
| Account at 4.25% (a high-yield rate) | about $425 | about $10,050, a gain of $50 |

(Example. Assumes rates and inflation hold steady for the year. Your rate and balance will differ.)

Same $10,000, same bank deposit insurance, same access. One version loses about $320 of buying power. The other stays a little ahead. The gap is roughly $390 a year in interest alone.

That is the difference between a savings account getting a raise and one taking a pay cut.

### It adds up while you build, too

The gap grows when you're adding money every month. Say you put $250 a month into your emergency fund for three years. That's $9,000 of your own money.

At 0.37%, you end with about $9,050, so interest added roughly $50. At 4.25%, you end with about $9,570, so interest added roughly $570. (Example. Assumes the rate stays the same for three years, which it won't, since savings rates are variable.)

No extra effort, no extra risk. Just a better spot for the same dollars to sit.

### Five steps to give your savings a raise

1. **Find your rate.** Log in to the bank that holds your savings and look up the APY. It's often buried, so search the account details. If it starts with a zero, you have room to improve.
2. **Compare a few high-yield accounts.** Online banks and credit unions usually pay the most. Check that the account is FDIC-insured (or NCUA-insured at a credit union), and read the fine print. Watch for minimum balances, monthly fees, and teaser rates that drop after a few months.
3. **Keep it boring and easy to reach.** An emergency fund is for emergencies, not for chasing returns. A savings account you can reach in a day or two is the right home. Investing belongs to money you won't need for years.
4. **Automate the deposit.** Set a transfer for the day after payday. Money that moves on its own gets saved. Money that waits for a good month usually doesn't.
5. **Check in monthly.** Savings rates are variable. They rose with the Fed, and they can fall if rates come back down. A quick look once a month tells you whether your account is still earning its keep.

One note at tax time: interest from a savings account is generally taxable income, so the bank will send you a form if you earn more than a small amount. A higher rate still leaves you ahead. Just don't be surprised by the 1099.
