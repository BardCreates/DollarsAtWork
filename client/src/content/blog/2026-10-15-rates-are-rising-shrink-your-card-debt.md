Prices went up faster in September. The Federal Reserve is already pushing rates higher to cool them down. If you carry a credit card balance, that news lands on your statement, not just in the headlines.

Here is the good part. You can't vote on the Fed's next move. You can decide where your next $150 goes. And that choice matters far more than a quarter-point hike.

### What happened this week

On October 14, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that consumer prices rose 3.7% over the past year. That's up from 3.4% in August. Prices jumped 0.6% in September alone, led by a 9.1% monthly rise in gasoline.

Paychecks aren't keeping up. Real average hourly earnings, meaning pay after inflation, fell 0.7% over the past year, according to Yahoo Finance's coverage of the report. One economist there put it bluntly: "It's a financial squeeze."

The Fed has noticed. On September 16 it raised its benchmark rate by a quarter point, to a range of 3.75% to 4.00%. It was the first hike since 2023. After the inflation report, futures traders briefly put the odds of another hike at the October 27–28 meeting near 50%, before they settled closer to one in three.

### Why a Fed hike shows up on your card

Most credit cards have a variable APR. That rate is tied to the prime rate, which moves when the Fed moves. So when the Fed goes up a quarter point, your card's rate usually follows within a billing cycle or two.

Card rates were already high before any of this. The Fed's own data put the average rate at 22.15% for accounts that paid interest in the second quarter. Americans also owed about $1.35 trillion in revolving credit in August, most of it on cards.

A rate rise doesn't change your balance overnight. It changes how fast the balance grows while you pay it down. That's a problem you can outwork.

### What a rate rise really costs (example)

Let's use a made-up household. They owe $10,000 on three cards:

| Card | Balance | APR | Minimum |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Store card | $1,200 | 26.99% | $40 |
| Visa | $3,500 | 22.9% | $105 |
| Mastercard | $5,300 | 20.5% | $159 |

They pay the same $304 every month. At those rates they're debt-free in about 54 months and pay roughly $5,545 in interest.

Now say rates climb another half point over the next few months. Same payments. They pay about $240 more in interest and finish a month later.

That's real money. But look at the next number.

### The bigger lever: a debt snowball

The debt snowball is simple. Pay the minimum on every debt. Put every extra dollar on the smallest balance. When it's gone, roll its whole payment onto the next smallest. The payment gets bigger each time, like a snowball rolling downhill.

Our example household finds $150 a month in their budget. They now put $454 a month toward the cards, aimed with the snowball. Here's what happens (example numbers):

- The store card is paid off in month 7. Its $40, plus the extra $150, rolls to the Visa.
- The Visa is gone in month 20. Now $295 a month hits the Mastercard, on top of its own minimum.
- The last card is paid off in month 29.

That's about 25 months sooner than before. Total interest drops to about $2,824, which saves roughly $2,700. Even with the extra half-point rate rise, the snowball plan adds only about $90 in interest, because the balances shrink faster.

In plain terms, the $150 a month is worth about ten times more than the rate hike costs. The Fed controls the rate. You control the payment.

A quick note on the avalanche method. It sends the extra money to the highest APR first instead of the smallest balance. It usually saves a bit more interest. In this example both methods pick the store card first, so they come out the same. Pick the one you'll stick with. Quick wins keep a lot of people going.

### Five things you can do this week

1. **List every debt in one place.** Balance, APR and minimum for each card and loan. Your statement or app has all three.
2. **Check your current APR.** If it went up after the September hike, you'll see it on your latest statement. Write it down so you can spot the next change.
3. **Find your extra dollars on purpose.** Give every dollar of income a job before the month starts, so the extra payment is planned, not leftover. Even $50 makes a difference.
4. **Call your card issuer.** Ask if they'll lower your rate. It doesn't always work, but it costs you a five-minute call.
5. **Look at a balance transfer carefully.** A 0% transfer offer can help if you can pay it off before the promo ends. Count the transfer fee, usually a few percent, and don't add new charges to the old card.

One more thing. Keep a small emergency cushion while you do this. Without one, the next car repair goes right back on the card.
