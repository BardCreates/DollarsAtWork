A letter from the IRS usually means a bill. This fall, for millions of people, it means the opposite. The IRS has started mailing a notice called CP321J to tell workers they may qualify for a new program that puts government money into their retirement account.

It's called the Saver's Match. It starts with the 2027 tax year, and the first deposits arrive in 2028. If your income is under the line, it could be the best return you ever get on $2,000.

### What the letter says

The Saver's Match comes from the SECURE 2.0 law passed in 2022. Starting in 2027, the federal government matches 50% of what eligible workers put into a retirement plan or IRA, on the first $2,000 of contributions. That's up to $1,000 a year per person. For a married couple filing jointly, each spouse can get their own match, so up to $2,000 a year for the household.

The money doesn't lower your tax bill. It's deposited straight into your retirement account, on top of what you put in. That's the big change from the Saver's Credit it replaces. The old credit only helped if you owed federal income tax. Plenty of people in this income range don't, so they got nothing. The match pays out either way.

You claim it by filing a new form, Form 8880-A, with your 2027 return in 2028. And it isn't a one-time bonus. The IRS describes it as a match for each tax year, so it can repeat every year you still qualify.

The letters are going to people who claimed the Saver's Credit on their 2025 return, or whose 2025 income was in the eligible range. No letter? You can still qualify. The letter is a heads-up, not a ticket.

### Who qualifies

The match depends on your modified adjusted gross income, or MAGI. Here are the lines for 2027, from the IRS:

| Filing status | Full 50% match | Smaller match | No match |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Married filing jointly | Up to $41,000 | $41,001 to $70,999 | $71,000 and up |
| Head of household | Up to $30,750 | $30,751 to $53,249 | $53,250 and up |
| Single | Up to $20,500 | $20,501 to $35,499 | $35,500 and up |

Between the full-match line and the no-match line, the match rate shrinks as income rises. The IRS will adjust these limits for inflation after 2027.

A few other rules. You have to be 18 by the end of the year, not a student under the tax code's definition, not claimed as a dependent on someone else's return, and a U.S. resident for tax purposes. There's no minimum contribution. The IRS's own example is $20 a month: $240 in, $120 added, $360 saved for the year.

One thing to watch: MAGI adds pre-tax retirement contributions back in. So a bigger 401(k) contribution won't pull your income under the line.

### What a 50% match is worth (an example)

Let's make up a couple. Sam and Jordan file jointly and earn $40,000 together. That's under the $41,000 line, so each of them gets the full match.

They each put $2,000 a year into an IRA. That's about $167 a month each, or $77 a paycheck if they're paid every two weeks. The Saver's Match adds $1,000 for each of them. So the household puts in $4,000 and ends the year with $6,000 saved. A 50% return, before the money grows at all.

Now give it time. Assume their accounts earn 7% a year, and to keep the math simple, treat the match as if it arrived through the year along with their own contributions. (In real life it lands after they file, so the true numbers would run a little lower.)

| Balance | Their $4,000 a year alone | With the $2,000 match |
| --- | --- | --- |
| After 10 years | about $57,000 | about $85,500 |
| After 20 years | about $169,000 | about $254,000 |
| After 30 years | about $390,000 | about $585,000 |

Over 30 years, the match adds roughly $195,000 to their balance. The government's share of that is $60,000. Growth did the rest.

Of course, most people's income rises. Say Sam and Jordan outgrow the match after five years. By then they'd have collected $10,000 of it. Left alone at 7%, that $10,000 would be worth about $64,000 at the 30-year mark. Five years of filling out one form, and it's still a used car's worth of retirement money.

These are example numbers at an assumed rate. Markets don't return 7% every year, and some years they go down. The point isn't the exact figure. It's that a 50% match on a modest amount, started early, is a big deal.

### Still paying off debt?

If you're carrying card balances at 22%, you might wonder whether to put anything toward retirement yet. That's a fair question, and the answer is yours to make.

Here's the math to make it with. A 50% match is a 50% return on the day it arrives. Paying down a 22% card is a sure 22% return. Both are great. Only one of them is capped at $2,000 a year, and only one goes away if you skip the year.

So many people split it: contribute enough to get the full match, then send every other spare dollar at the debt. If you also have a workplace plan that matches, say, 50% of what you put in up to 6% of your pay (a typical design, according to Vanguard), the same contribution can earn the employer match and the Saver's Match. Two matches on one deposit.

### What to do before January

1. **Check your income against the table.** Use last year's return as a guide. If you're in the full or partial range, this applies to you.
2. **Pick the account.** A 401(k), 403(b) or governmental 457(b) at work counts, and so does a traditional or Roth IRA. The IRS is still writing the rules for how deposits reach workplace plans, and employers can choose whether their plan takes them. If you have no account, the IRS says TrumpIRA.gov will list IRA providers that accept the match when it launches on January 1, 2027.
3. **Set the contribution up now.** $2,000 a year is $167 a month. Give it a line in your January plan so it's planned, not leftover.
4. **Nothing to claim for 2026.** The Saver's Credit still exists for this year's return, if you qualify. The match begins with 2027 contributions.
5. **Keep records.** Statements showing what you put in during 2027. You'll claim the match on Form 8880-A in 2028.
6. **Leave it alone.** Withdrawals before 59½ can bring extra taxes. The match is retirement money.
