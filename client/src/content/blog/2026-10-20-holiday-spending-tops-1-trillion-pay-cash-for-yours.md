The holidays have a date. Your card bill has one too, about three weeks later. This week two big forecasts came out about how much Americans will spend between now and New Year's, and the number is the biggest ever. That's not a reason to panic. It's a reason to decide, today, how your share gets paid.

Here's the plan: pick a number, pay most of it in cash this year, and set up a fund so next year pays for itself.

### What came out this week

On October 15, the National Retail Federation said it expects holiday sales in November and December to reach $1.013 trillion, up 4.2% from last year's $972 billion. That's faster than the usual 3% pace, and the NRF says about half of the growth is simply higher prices, not more stuff. Gas was $4.33 a gallon the day the forecast landed, according to AAA, and the latest inflation report showed prices rising 3.7% over the past year.

The same day, Deloitte released its yearly holiday survey of 4,000 shoppers. The average household plans to spend $1,654 this season, up 4% from $1,595 last year. Gifts and other goods take $988 of that; the rest goes to travel, entertaining and other holiday activities. People are starting early, too: 28% expect their holiday budget to be spent by the end of October, the highest share in six years.

One more number to keep in mind. The Federal Reserve's latest report puts the average rate on credit cards that carry a balance at 22.36%. That's the rate a holiday charges if it sits on a card.

### Why "I'll pay it off in January" costs more than it looks

Say you put $1,000 of holiday on a card at 22.36%. (This is an example; your rate and balance will differ.)

| What you send each month | Paid off in | Interest paid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $25 | about 6 years | about $850 |
| $50 | 26 months | about $263 |
| $100 | 12 months | about $117 |
| $250 | 5 months | about $49 |

At $50 a month, the gifts cost 26% more than the price tag. At $25 a month, they're still on the bill six Christmases later.

There's a bigger catch if you're already paying off debt. New charges go to the back of the line. Another example: say you owe $14,000 across your cards at around 20% and you're sending $500 a month at it. Alone, you're debt-free in 39 months. Add a $1,200 holiday to the pile and the date moves to 43 months, and because that $1,200 waits its turn behind everything else, it collects about $1,160 of interest before it's gone. The gifts nearly double in price, and your debt-free date slips four months.

So the question isn't "can I afford the holidays?" It's "how do I keep them off the 22% card?"

### A five-week plan for this year

Black Friday is November 27. That gives you about five weeks and, if you're paid every two weeks, two or three paychecks. Not enough to save the whole thing from scratch, but enough to change the ending.

Here's an example. Priya decides on $1,000 for the holidays: $650 for gifts, $200 for travel, $150 for food and the office party. She writes the list first, with a number next to each name, so the total is a decision and not a surprise.

She gets paid every two weeks, so she gives "Holidays" a line in her plan and sends $250 from each of the next two paychecks to it. That's $500 in cash by the week of Thanksgiving. The other $500 goes on her card in December, but she already knows how it leaves: $250 in January and $250 in February. That costs her about $14 of interest, total, and she's clear by March.

Compare that with the $1,000 at $50 a month above: 26 months and $263. Same holiday, same gifts. The difference is that Priya decided the number in October and the exit in October.

Two things that make this easier. Shop the number, not the sale: a deal on something that wasn't on the list isn't savings. And cap the self-gift: Deloitte found shoppers plan to spend $299 on themselves this year. Yours could be the $299 that never touched a card.

### Next year: a fund that fills itself

The real fix is the one that takes a year. The holidays aren't a surprise. They're a bill that comes back every December, like car insurance or the water heater's eventual funeral. Bills that come back get a fund.

Here's how the math looks for a $1,000 holiday, saving a little each month from January. (Example again: assumes a high-yield savings account paying 4% APY, which is around what the best accounts pay right now, with the balance rounded.)

| Each month | After 12 months |
| --- | --- |
| $85 | about $1,040 |
| $100 | about $1,220 |
| $140 | about $1,710, close to Deloitte's average household |

The interest is small, around $20 to $40 a year. That's not the point. The point is what you don't pay: the $117 to $850 from the first table. The $85 a month comes out of your paycheck either way. You're choosing whether it goes out before the holidays, into an account that pays you, or after them, to a card that charges you.

In December the fund pays the bill, and in January it starts filling again.

### What to do this week

1. **Pick your total.** Write the list, put a number by each name, add travel and food. Round it to something you'd say out loud.
2. **Give it a line in your plan.** "Holidays" gets a monthly amount like rent and groceries do. If the plan goes over your income, something else gives, and it's better to know now.
3. **Decide the exit for anything that lands on a card.** How much a month, and which month it's gone. Write it down.
4. **If you're paying off debt, protect your date.** Fund the holidays from a cash line, not from the money you send at your snowball. A smaller holiday that keeps your debt-free date is a gift to future you.
5. **Open the holiday fund in January.** One deposit a month, at a bank that pays a real rate. Next October, a forecast like this one is just news.
