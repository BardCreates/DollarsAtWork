You've heard the coffee lecture. Skip the latte, retire a millionaire. It's annoying, and it's half right. The small stuff does add up. It's also true that a coffee never made anyone broke, and nobody should feel bad about a $5 cup.

So this post isn't a lecture. It's the math, worked out, so you can put a price on a habit and then decide for yourself. Some habits are worth every dollar. Some you don't even notice. The trick is knowing which is which.

### How much the small stuff adds up to

The Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks what the average American household spends each year. Its latest figures, for 2024, put food away from home at $3,945 a year. That's restaurants, takeout, delivery, the lunch run and the coffee. Call it $329 a month.

That number is an average across every household, so yours could be half of it or double it. And a lot of it isn't a "habit" at all. Dinner with friends, a birthday, the night you were too tired to cook: that's life, and it belongs in the plan.

Subscriptions are the quieter kind. A survey of 1,272 US adults for Self Financial in March 2026 found people with paid subscriptions average 3.4 of them, and about 60% had at least one they hadn't used in the past 30 days. The unused ones averaged $26.79 a month. That's a habit nobody chose. It just kept renewing.

Add a few of those together and $5 a day is easy to find. $5 a day is about $150 a month, or $1,800 a year. Here's what that amount is worth, three different ways.

### The three prices of $150 a month

This is an example with assumed rates, not a forecast. Your numbers will differ.

**Price one: invested.** Say the $150 goes into an index fund every month and earns 7% a year, which is a common long-run figure for stocks (an assumption, not a promise).

| After | You put in | It could be worth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 years | $18,000 | about $25,700 |
| 20 years | $36,000 | about $76,100 |
| 30 years | $54,000 | about $175,400 |

The first ten years are mostly your own money. The last ten are mostly growth. That's compounding, and it's why the lecture exists.

**Price two: sent at a card.** Say you owe $8,000 on a credit card at 22.36%, the average rate on cards that carry a balance in the Federal Reserve's latest report, and you're paying $250 a month. Alone, that takes 50 months and about $4,280 of interest. Add the $150 and pay $400 a month instead: 26 months and about $2,100 of interest. The habit, pointed at the card, is worth two years and about $2,180.

**Price three: saved.** In a savings account paying 4%, $150 a month is about $1,830 after one year. That's a starter emergency fund, the kind that turns a flat tire from a card charge into a bill you just pay.

Three prices, one habit. None of them is "right." They're what you give up, so you can decide if the habit is worth more than that to you.

### The part the coffee lecture leaves out

A $150 habit will not fix a $400 rent problem. If your plan doesn't close no matter what you cut, the answer is the big lines: housing, the car payment, income. Nobody saves their way out of those with a thermos. Pricing a habit is for after the big lines are set, when you're deciding what the last few hundred dollars do.

Keep the habits you love. If the Saturday coffee with a friend is the best $5 of your week, that's a good buy, and knowing its price doesn't change that. The ones to price are the ones you don't notice: the subscription you forgot, the lunch out because you didn't plan one, the third delivery fee this week.

And watch where the habit lands. If it goes on a card that already carries a balance, it costs more than its price tag. A year of $150-a-month habits is $1,800. Pay that off at $50 a month at 22.36% and it takes 61 months and about $1,210 of interest. On the card, the habit costs about $3,000, not $1,800. Paid from your plan, it costs $1,800.

### A worked example: Sam finds $150

Sam is an example, not a real person. Sam has an $8,000 card balance at 22.36% and sends $250 a month at it. Sam also eats lunch out most workdays and hasn't opened two of his streaming apps since the summer.

Sam doesn't go cold turkey. He cancels the two apps ($40 a month) and takes lunch from four days a week to two, which saves about $110. That's $150 a month, found without giving up the thing he actually likes, which is Friday lunch with his team.

He gives the $150 a job the same day: it goes to the card. His card payment is now $400. Alone, Sam's debt-free date was 50 months out. Now it's 26 months. He keeps about $2,180 that would have gone to interest.

When the card is gone, the $400 doesn't vanish. It needs a new job, and Sam already picked one: $150 to a starter emergency fund until it hits $1,800, then all of it to his 401(k). At 7%, that $400 a month could be about $68,000 in ten years. The habit that started this was two forgotten apps and some sandwiches.

### How to price your own habits

1. **List, don't judge.** Write down what you spent in the last month on food out, delivery, subscriptions and small stuff. Just the list. No deciding yet.
2. **Pick one or two.** Choose the ones you'd miss least. Leave the one you love alone.
3. **Give the freed dollars a job the same day.** Debt, savings or investing. Money with no job drifts back to where it came from.
4. **Pay the habit from your plan, not your card.** A habit with a line in the budget costs its price. A habit on a card costs its price plus 22%.
5. **Check again next month.** Did the money go where you sent it? Did you miss the habit? If you did, buy it back. The plan is yours.
