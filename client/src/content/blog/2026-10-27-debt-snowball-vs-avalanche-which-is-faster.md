Debt snowball vs. avalanche: which is faster? With the same monthly payment, the avalanche method is usually a little faster, often by a month or less, and it pays less interest. The snowball method gets your first debt paid off sooner, and in the research, that first win is what keeps people going.

That's the honest answer, and it's smaller than the internet makes it sound. The thing that actually decides your debt-free date is how much you send each month, not which order you send it in. Here's the math on three real-looking debts, so you can see the gap for yourself and then decide.

### What the two methods are

Both methods start the same way. You pay the minimum on every debt, every month. Then you take whatever extra you can afford and send all of it to one debt at a time. When that debt is gone, its payment joins the extra and rolls onto the next one. The only difference is the order.

**Snowball:** smallest balance first, whatever its rate. You clear the little ones fast and the pile of accounts shrinks.

**Avalanche:** highest interest rate first, whatever its balance. Every dollar goes where it's being charged the most, so you pay the least interest.

Both work. Neither is fast on its own. The speed comes from the rollover: a paid-off debt's payment never goes back into your pocket until every debt is gone.

### A worked example: three debts, $770 a month

This is an example with made-up balances and assumed rates, not a real person. The card rate is the average on cards that carry a balance, 22.36%, from the Federal Reserve's latest figure (August 2026).

| Debt | Balance | Rate | Minimum |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Car loan | $2,400 | 6.9% | $180 |
| Store card | $3,100 | 28.99% | $95 |
| Credit card | $9,800 | 22.36% | $245 |

That's $15,300 owed and $520 a month in minimums. Say you can find $250 more, so $770 a month goes to debt until it's done.

**Snowball** sends the extra to the car loan first (smallest balance), then the store card, then the credit card.

**Avalanche** sends it to the store card first (highest rate), then the credit card, then the car loan.

| | Snowball | Avalanche |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First debt paid off | Car loan, month 6 | Store card, month 11 |
| Debt-free | Month 26 | Month 25 |
| Interest paid | About $4,055 | About $3,797 |

So avalanche wins by one month and about $260. Over two years, that's roughly $10 a month. Snowball hands you a paid-off account five months sooner, and the car loan's $180 starts rolling onto the next debt in month 7.

Now the number that matters more than either column. Paying only the minimums, with no extra and no rollover, takes 75 months and about $11,535 of interest. The $250 extra, in either order, saves four years and about $7,500. The method chose $260. The extra chose $7,500.

One more comparison: snowball at $820 a month (that's $50 more) is debt-free in 24 months with about $3,737 of interest. That beats avalanche at $770 on both counts. Finding $50 did more than switching methods.

### When the gap gets big

The gap between the methods grows when your smallest debt has a low rate and your biggest debt has a high one, because snowball spends months on the cheap debt while the expensive one keeps charging.

Another example, same assumptions: a $4,000 student loan at 5.5% ($90 minimum) and a $14,000 credit card at 24.99% ($350 minimum), with $690 a month going to debt. Snowball is debt-free in 37 months with about $7,382 of interest. Avalanche takes 35 months and about $5,800. Now the gap is two months and about $1,580, and the snowball's "quick win" means 13 months on the loan while the card charges 25%.

When the rates are close, the gap nearly disappears. Two cards at 21% and 23%, with the same kind of extra, come out about $60 and one month apart.

Rule of thumb: the wider the gap between your rates, and the longer snowball would park you on the cheap debt, the more avalanche saves. If the rates are within a few points of each other, pick by what keeps you going, because the math barely cares.

### Why the first win matters

Two studies looked at how people actually pay off debt, not how they should on paper.

In 2012, David Gal and Blakeley McShane at Northwestern's Kellogg School studied records from about 6,000 people in a debt settlement program. People who closed out their smallest balances first were more likely to clear all of their debt. It was the closing of accounts, not the size of them, that predicted finishing.

In 2016, Keri Kettle, Rémi Trudel, Simon Blanchard and Gerald Häubl published experiments and an analysis of credit data in the Journal of Consumer Research. People who concentrated their payments on one account at a time paid down their card debt about 15% faster than people who spread the same money across all their accounts. What motivated them was the share of a balance they'd knocked out, which is why a small debt feels so good to finish.

Put the two together: pick one debt and pile on, in any order. The pile is the method. The order is a tweak, and it's worth a few hundred dollars one way and some early momentum the other.

### How to pick, in five minutes

1. **List every debt** with its balance, rate and minimum payment. The statement or the app has all three.
2. **Add up the minimums.** That's your floor. Anything above it is the extra.
3. **Find the extra.** Even $50 a month matters more than the order. The plan is where it comes from, and a habit line is often the first place to look.
4. **Check the rate gap.** Highest rate minus lowest. Wide, with a big balance at the low rate? Lean avalanche. Close together? Lean snowball.
5. **Pick and start this month.** Both methods only work if the extra actually goes out. Set the payment up so it happens without you.

## Common questions

**Does the snowball method save money?** No. With the same monthly payment, it costs a little more interest than avalanche, because the high-rate debt waits longer. In the example above the cost is about $260 over two years. The thing it buys is an earlier paid-off account.

**Can I mix the two?** Yes, and a lot of people do: snowball one tiny balance to feel the win, then avalanche the rest. Or avalanche everything except a store card that's small enough to clear in a couple of months. The math doesn't mind, as long as the extra lands on one debt at a time.

**Should I include my mortgage or car loan?** A car loan with a few years left usually belongs in the order. A mortgage usually doesn't, because its rate is far below a card's and its balance would freeze the rollover for decades. Pay its minimum, keep it out of the order and decide separately whether extra principal is worth it.

**What about a 0% balance transfer?** A promotional rate is the lowest rate you have, so avalanche puts it last. The catch is the date the promotion ends. If the minimum won't clear the balance by then, treat the rate it jumps to as its real rate and move it up the order so it's gone before the deadline.

**What if I can't afford more than the minimums?** Then the order doesn't matter yet, and neither method will finish on its own schedule. The move is to find extra, even a little, or to raise income. Both methods are tools for the extra dollars; until there are some, the job is finding them.
