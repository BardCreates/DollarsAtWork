How much should your emergency fund be if you have debt? If the debt is high-rate, like a credit card, keep a starter fund of about $1,000 to one month of must-pay bills, send everything else at the debt, and build the full three to six months once the cards are gone. If the debt is low-rate, like a car loan or a federal student loan, you can build the bigger fund alongside it.

That's the short version, and most articles stop there. The part they skip is the price. A starter fund costs you a few months and some interest; a full fund while you carry a 22% card costs a lot more. Here's the arithmetic on one made-up person, so you can see both prices and pick the size that fits you.

### Why the usual "3 to 6 months" rule doesn't fit yet

The three-to-six-months rule is a good target. It's just written for someone who isn't paying a card 22% at the same time.

Picture $8,500 in savings at 4% while $9,000 sits on a card at 22.36%, the average rate on cards that carry a balance in the Federal Reserve's latest figure (August 2026). The savings earn about $340 a year. The card charges about $2,000 a year. Holding both costs roughly $1,550 a year compared with sending the savings at the card: the gap between the two rates, about 18 points, times the money you're holding.

So while the high-rate debt exists, the full fund is expensive. The question becomes: how small can the fund be and still keep an emergency off the card?

### The two stages

**Stage one, while you owe on cards: a starter fund.** Enough for the emergencies that happen in a normal year: a tire, a copay, an insurance deductible, a vet bill. For most people that's $1,000 to about one month of must-pay bills. Lean high if your income moves around, you own a home or your car is old. Lean low if you rent, your paycheck is steady and your deductibles are small.

**Stage two, once the high-rate debt is gone: the full fund.** Three to six months of must-pay bills. Not everything you spend, just the bills that keep coming if your income stopped: housing, utilities, groceries, transportation, insurance, minimum payments. Six months if one income covers the household or your work is seasonal; three if you have two steady incomes.

The nice surprise about stage two: your must-pay number shrinks when the cards are gone, because the minimums drop out of it.

### A worked example: Jordan, $9,000 on a card

Jordan is an example with made-up numbers, not a real person. Jordan takes home $4,200 a month and owes $9,000 on one card at 22.36%, with a $225 minimum. Jordan's must-pay bills:

| Bill | A month |
| --- | --- |
| Rent | $1,400 |
| Groceries | $450 |
| Car payment | $210 |
| Utilities | $180 |
| Gas | $160 |
| Insurance | $140 |
| Phone | $60 |
| Card minimum | $225 |
| **Total** | **$2,825** |

After those bills and some ordinary living, Jordan has $400 a month free. Savings: $0.

**Stage one target.** Jordan rents, has a steady paycheck and a $500 car insurance deductible. A $1,000 starter fund covers the deductible and a repair on top. One month of bills would be $2,825; Jordan picks $1,000 and the faster payoff.

**Stage two target.** Once the card is gone, the minimum leaves the table and the must-pay bills are $2,600. Three months is $7,800. Six months is $15,600. Jordan aims for three months first.

### What the starter fund costs

Every $400 that goes to savings is $400 that didn't go at the 22% card. Here's the card with and without the starter fund, assuming the rate stays put.

| Plan | Card paid off | Interest paid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $625 a month at the card from day one | Month 17 | About $1,580 |
| Save $1,000 first (2.5 months), then $625 at the card | Month 20 | About $1,940 |

The starter fund costs Jordan about three months and about $360 in extra interest. Spread over the payoff, that's about $15 a month. Think of it as an insurance premium.

What does the premium buy? Say a $1,000 car repair lands in month 8. Without a fund, it goes on the card: the payoff slips to month 19 and the interest to about $1,810. With the fund, Jordan pays the shop from savings, refills the $1,000 over the next two and a half months, and the card is gone in month 22 with about $2,180 of interest.

Read that honestly: on interest alone, the card is the cheaper emergency fund, by roughly $370 over two years. What the starter fund buys is what the arithmetic can't see. The repair doesn't depend on the card having room that month. You don't watch a balance you've fought for eight months jump back up. And if the emergency is a lost job, cash covers rent and a card doesn't. In the Federal Reserve's latest household survey, 63% of adults said they could cover a $400 surprise with cash or its equivalent; the rest would borrow, sell something or couldn't pay it at all. Asked about a $1,000 surprise in a Bankrate survey published in January, 30% said they'd pay it from savings and 17% said they'd put it on a card and pay it off over time. That second group is the one the starter fund keeps you out of.

### How to set your own number, in ten minutes

1. **List your must-pay bills** for one month: housing, utilities, groceries, transportation, insurance, phone and every debt minimum. Nothing else.
2. **Write down your likely emergencies:** deductibles, your car's age, what broke last year. Take the biggest one and add a cushion.
3. **Pick your starter target.** The bigger of $1,000 and that emergency total, up to one month of bills.
4. **Send everything else at the highest-rate debt** until it's gone. The starter fund is a pause, not a destination; the shorter the pause, the less it costs.
5. **Set the stage two target now,** three to six months of bills without the minimums, so the day the card is paid off the $625 already has a job.
6. **Put the fund where it earns something.** The average savings account pays 0.37%; some high-yield accounts pay around 4%. On $1,000 that's $4 a year versus $40. Small, but free.

## Common questions

**Should I pay off debt or save an emergency fund first?** A small fund first, then the debt, then the big fund. Going straight at the debt with nothing saved works right up until the first surprise, which then goes on the card. Saving the full fund first while a card charges 22% costs more than almost any emergency would.

**Is $1,000 enough?** It covers the common stuff: a repair, a deductible, a copay. It won't cover a month without a paycheck. If your income is uneven, you have kids or you own a home, lean toward one month of bills. Size it to the emergencies you actually have, not to a round number.

**What if my debt is low-rate?** A car loan at 7% or a federal student loan at 5% changes the math. Holding $8,000 at 4% against a 7% loan costs about $240 a year, not $1,550. At that price many people build the full fund and pay the loan on schedule at the same time. The urgency comes from the rate, not from the word "debt."

**What counts as an emergency?** Something you didn't choose and can't delay: the car won't start, the tooth hurts, the paycheck didn't come. A trip, Christmas and a new phone are expenses; a sinking fund is the place for them. If you keep raiding the emergency fund for predictable things, the fix is more categories in your plan, not a bigger fund.

**After I use the fund, do I refill it or go back to the debt?** Refill first, as fast as you can, then return to the extra payments. In Jordan's example that's two and a half months. A fund you don't refill is a windfall you already spent.
