What is a retirement number, and how do you find yours? It's the amount of invested money that could pay for your retirement, and the common way to find it is to take a year of the spending your savings must cover, after Social Security or a pension, and multiply it by 25. Spend $1,800 a month from savings and your number is about $540,000.

That's the short answer. The longer one is where the 25 comes from, what to feed into it, and how to tell whether your monthly investing gets you there by the age you have in mind. Here's all of it, with one made-up person and real arithmetic.

### Where the 25 comes from

In 1994 a financial planner named William Bengen tested a question against US market history back to 1926: what's the most a retiree could take out of a stock-and-bond portfolio in year one, then raise that amount with inflation every year, and still not run out in 30 years? In the worst stretch he found, about 4%. A 1998 paper by three Trinity University professors backed it up, and the "4% rule" was born.

Flip 4% over and you get 25. If you can safely spend 4% of a pot in the first year, the pot needs to be 25 times what you spend. That's the whole rule of 25.

Two footnotes. The rule came from one country's past, and the future can differ: Morningstar's research for 2026 puts the safe starting rate at 3.9% for a 30-year retirement with a 90% chance of success, while Bengen, in a 2025 book, raised his own figure to 4.7% with a more diversified portfolio. Those give multipliers from about 21 to about 26. And the rule assumes your spending keeps pace with inflation and never dips; retirees who can cut back in a bad year can usually start higher. Treat 25 as a good middle, not a law.

### The four inputs that set your number

1. **What you'd spend in a month of retirement, in today's dollars.** Start with what you spend now on everything except saving, investing and debt payments. Then adjust: a paid-off mortgage drops out, commuting shrinks, health care usually grows. If you have no idea, use today's everyday spending. It's a better guess than a percentage of salary.
2. **What arrives without touching savings.** Social Security, a pension, part-time work you'd like. Your Social Security statement shows an estimate; the Social Security Administration's estimate for the average retired worker at the end of 2026 is $2,094 a month, so if you're guessing, start near there and go lower for a spotty work history.
3. **The gap.** Spending minus that income. This is the only part your savings has to carry.
4. **Times 12, times 25.** The gap for a year, multiplied by 25.

Notice what isn't on the list: your salary. Rules like "10 times your salary by 67" (Fidelity's guideline, with 1x by 30, 3x by 40, 6x by 50 and 8x by 60) are fine as a glance in the mirror, but two people on the same salary can spend very differently, and spending is what you'll have to pay for.

### A worked example: Maya, 32, $500 a month

Maya is an example with made-up numbers, not a real person. She takes home $4,600 a month, spends $3,800 of it on everything but saving and investing, has $30,000 in a 401(k), and puts in $500 a month counting her employer's match. She'd like to retire at 65.

**Spending in retirement:** she expects to live about like she does now, so $3,800 a month in today's dollars.

**Other income:** her Social Security statement estimates about $2,000 a month.

**Gap:** $3,800 − $2,000 = $1,800 a month, or $21,600 a year.

**Number:** $21,600 × 25 = **$540,000**, in today's dollars.

Check it the other way: 4% of $540,000 is $21,600, the first year's withdrawals. It matches.

Had Maya skipped Social Security, her number would have been $3,800 × 12 × 25 = $1,140,000, more than double. That's a common mistake in retirement number articles: they multiply all of your spending, not the gap, and hand you a number that feels impossible.

### Does $500 a month get there?

Here's the part the calculators leave out. Assume a 7% yearly return, 3% inflation, and that Maya raises her $500 with her pay so it stays $500 in today's dollars. Everything below is in today's dollars, and it's an estimate, not a promise.

| Maya invests | Hits $540,000 at about | Has at 65 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $500 a month | 66 and a half | About $500,000 |
| $650 a month | 62 | About $618,000 |
| $800 a month | 59 | About $737,000 |

So $500 a month gets her close: about $40,000 short at 65, and across the line a year and a half later. The surprise is how little closes the gap. About **$551 a month** reaches $540,000 right at 65. In this example, $51 a month, found once, is the difference between reaching the number at 65 and at 66 and a half.

One more number worth seeing. If Maya never raises the $500, not even with inflation, she has about $383,000 at 65 in today's money instead of $500,000. Contributions that don't grow with your pay quietly shrink every year. The fix is a habit, not heroics: when your pay goes up, your investing goes up.

### How to find your number in ten minutes

1. **Add up a month of everyday spending.** Rent or mortgage, groceries, utilities, transportation, insurance, phone, fun. Leave out savings, investing and debt payments. Adjust for the retirement you picture, or don't; today's number is a fine start.
2. **Look up your Social Security estimate** on your statement, and any pension. If you plan to retire before those start, your savings carry the whole bill for those years, so plan a bigger cushion or a later date.
3. **Subtract, then multiply.** (Spending − other income) × 12 × 25. Write it down. That's your number, in today's dollars.
4. **Check your pace.** Take what you invest now, including any match, and project it at a return you believe (7% is common; 6% is safer) less inflation. If it lands short, find the monthly amount that doesn't. It's usually smaller than you fear.
5. **Revisit once a year.** Spending changes, pay changes, the rules get updated. A number you check beats a number you set once.

## Common questions

**Should I pay off debt before saving for retirement?** Take any employer match first; it's an instant 50% or 100% return that no card rate beats. Then clear high-rate debt, 20% cards especially, before investing much beyond the match. Low-rate debt like a 6% car loan can sit alongside retirement investing. [Debt snowball vs. avalanche: which is faster](/blog/debt-snowball-vs-avalanche-which-is-faster) covers the payoff side.

**Does my employer match count toward the monthly amount?** Yes. Maya's $500 includes hers. The match is money going into your account, and it compounds the same as yours.

**Is 25 too many, or not enough?** It depends on how long retirement is and how flexible you are. Retire at 45 and a 40- or 50-year horizon argues for closer to 30. Retire at 70 with Social Security covering most of your spending and 20 might do. Morningstar's 3.9% says 25.6; Bengen's 4.7% says 21.3. Pick 25, then nudge for your situation.

**What about taxes?** Withdrawals from a traditional 401(k) or IRA are taxed as income; Roth withdrawals aren't. If most of your money is in traditional accounts, either add your expected tax to the spending figure or aim a little above your number.

**What if I'm 45 and nowhere near it?** The number doesn't care how old you are; the monthly amount does. Run step 4 with your real years left. A shorter runway needs more a month, but a later date, a few years of part-time work, or a smaller spending figure each pull the number toward you. Pick the lever you can live with.
