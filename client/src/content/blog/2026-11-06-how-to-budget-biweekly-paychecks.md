How do you budget biweekly paychecks when your bills come once a month? Build your monthly plan on two paychecks, because that's what ten months of the year bring, and give the two extra checks a year a job before they arrive. If you'd rather budget your true average month, multiply one paycheck by 26 and divide by 12, and keep a cushion for the months that only bring two.

That's the whole method. What most guides skip is what those two extra checks are worth, so here's a made-up person, both ways of doing it, and the arithmetic.

### Why biweekly pay never lines up with the month

Paid every two weeks, you get 26 paychecks a year. There are 12 months. Twenty-four of the checks fit two to a month, and the other two land in months that end up with three paydays. Which months those are depends on your payday: for a Friday schedule, 2026's three-paycheck months were January and July or May and October, and 2027's are April and October, or January, July and December. (A year with 27 paydays comes around about once every 11 years, and 2027 is one for people whose first 2027 payday is January 1.)

So the problem has a simple shape: two months a year, a check shows up that your monthly bills don't need. Put a number on it. Two extra paychecks add up to exactly one month of your two-paycheck budget, hiding inside the year.

### Two ways to turn paychecks into a monthly plan

**The two-paycheck month.** Your monthly income is one paycheck times two. That's what ten months bring, so a plan built on it works every month without a cushion. The two extra checks are one-time money: they're not in the plan, so they need a job of their own, decided ahead of time.

**The averaged month.** Your monthly income is one paycheck times 26, divided by 12. It's your real average, so a plan built on it puts every dollar to work evenly. The catch: ten months a year, the cash that arrives is less than the plan, and you make up the gap in the three-paycheck months. That works if you keep about one paycheck sitting in checking as a buffer. Without one, a two-paycheck month can come up short.

| | Two-paycheck month | Averaged month |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly income in the plan | Paycheck × 2 | Paycheck × 26 ÷ 12 |
| Matches the cash most months? | Yes | No, short by a sixth of a paycheck |
| Needs a buffer in checking? | No | About one paycheck |
| Where the extra checks go | One-time money, planned ahead | Already spread across the year |

Pick the two-paycheck month if your checking account runs close to zero or you've never budgeted before. Pick the averaged month once you have a paycheck's worth of cushion. Same money either way; the difference is timing, and in the example below it's small.

### A worked example: Jordan, $1,700 every two weeks

Jordan is an example with made-up numbers, not a real person. Take-home pay is $1,700 every other Friday, with the next three-paycheck months in April and October 2027. Two debts: a credit card at $6,400 and 22.36% (the Federal Reserve's latest average rate on cards that carry a balance, August 2026), minimum $190, and a car loan at $9,200 and an assumed 7.5%, minimum $265.

**The two-paycheck month:** $1,700 × 2 = $3,400.

| Category | A month |
| --- | --- |
| Rent | $1,200 |
| Groceries | $500 |
| Transportation (gas, insurance) | $300 |
| Utilities | $200 |
| Everything else (phone, health, fun) | $550 |
| Savings | $100 |
| Debt | $550 |
| **Total** | **$3,400** |

Debt gets $550: the $455 of minimums plus $95 extra, snowball order, so the card goes first. Every dollar has a job and Remaining is $0.

**The averaged month:** $1,700 × 26 = $44,200 a year, ÷ 12 = $3,683.33. That's $283.33 more a month than the two-paycheck plan, and Jordan would send it all to Debt, making the debt line $833.33.

**The extra checks:** two a year, $1,700 each, $3,400 in all. Exactly one two-paycheck month, as promised.

Now the part the step lists leave out: what happens to the debt-free date. These are estimates from the plan above, with the same rates throughout and nothing else changing.

| What Jordan does with the extra checks | Card paid off | Debt-free | Interest paid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spends them (two-paycheck plan, $550 to debt) | May 2029 | October 2029 | about $3,130 |
| Sends half of each to debt | May 2028 | February 2029 | about $2,250 |
| Sends all of each to debt | Jan 2028 | October 2028 | about $1,810 |
| Averaged month, $833 to debt every month | Dec 2027 | August 2028 | about $1,670 |

Two paychecks a year, sent to the card, move the debt-free date up a full year and save about $1,300 of interest. The averaged month comes in two months sooner still, because the money arrives a little earlier and more evenly, but it's the same $3,400 a year doing the work. And the card, the expensive one, is gone sixteen months sooner. That's what the third checks are really worth here: not $1,700 each, but a year.

One more row worth knowing. If Jordan paid only the minimums and spent the extras, the debts would run to August 2030 and cost about $4,770 of interest. Minimums plus the two extra checks, with nothing more a month, still finishes in December 2028. In this example the extra checks are a bigger lever than $95 a month.

### Lining up bills with paydays

The second half of the biweekly problem is timing, not totals. Rent is due on the 1st; one month the paycheck lands on the 2nd. Three fixes, cheapest first.

1. **Make a bill calendar.** List every bill with its due date and amount, then mark which paycheck covers it. Usually the bills due in the first half of the month fit the first check and the rest fit the second, with one or two awkward ones.
2. **Move the awkward due dates.** Card issuers, utilities and many lenders will shift a due date if you ask. Moving one bill is often enough.
3. **Build a one-paycheck buffer.** Keep one paycheck's worth in checking and the timing stops mattering: every bill is paid from money that's already there. The simplest way to build it is to send the next third paycheck to checking and leave it. After that, the averaged month becomes an option too.

The buffer is step three because it costs a third check, and in the example above each third check bought about three months of debt-free date. Buy the buffer first if a bounced payment is a real risk for you; otherwise send one extra check to the debt and the next to the buffer. Either order works. Deciding before the check lands is what matters.

### The third paycheck: decide before it lands

The third check isn't a bonus. It's a paycheck your bills don't need, and money without a job finds one on its own, usually in the week it arrives.

So give it a job in writing, now, for both of next year's dates. One sensible order, the same one that works for any extra money: a $1,000 starter emergency fund if you don't have one, then the checking buffer, then the debt at the front of your payoff order, then whatever goal is next. [How much should my emergency fund be if I have debt](/blog/how-much-should-my-emergency-fund-be-if-i-have-debt) walks through the starter-fund-then-debt order with the math, and [debt snowball vs. avalanche](/blog/debt-snowball-vs-avalanche-which-is-faster) covers which debt the money should hit.

And write the dates on the calendar. For Jordan, "April 30 and October 29: $1,700 to the card" is a plan. "I'll do something smart with it" is how it becomes a couch.

## Common questions

**Which months have three paychecks in 2027?** For a Friday payday, April and October if your first 2027 payday is January 8, and January, July and December if it's January 1 (that's the 27-paycheck year). Count it yourself in a minute: write your next payday on a calendar and keep adding 14 days; any month with three marks is one.

**Should I budget per paycheck or per month?** Per month, with a bill calendar to say which paycheck pays what. Two budgets a month is twice the work, and it hides what the whole month costs, which is the number your debt-free date depends on.

**What if I'm paid twice a month instead of every two weeks?** Then you get 24 checks, two every month, and there's no third check. Your monthly income is one paycheck times two, done. The bill-timing fixes above still apply.

**What do I do in a 27-paycheck year?** Nothing special: it's a third three-paycheck month, so it's a third extra check with the same job as the other two. If you use the averaged month, keep using 26; the 27th check is a one-off, and one-time money is what it is.

**My partner is paid monthly. How do we combine them?** Add each paycheck as its own income source with its own schedule and let the plan carry the total. The two-paycheck-month approach still works: plan on their monthly check plus two of yours, and your extras are the couple's one-time money. [How to budget as a couple](/blog/how-to-budget-as-a-couple-one-plan-two-paychecks) is the long version.
